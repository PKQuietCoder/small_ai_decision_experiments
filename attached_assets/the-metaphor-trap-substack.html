Borrowed Intuitions

Change one word, change the verdict (unless you're an AI)

Call crime a "beast" and people reach for the police. Call it a "virus" and they reach for reform. We ran that trick on six AI models. They didn't flinch.

A plain-language take on one of our experiments. The full report, with every number and the code to re-run it, lives on the site.

Same facts. One word. Different you.

Read this: crime is a beast preying on the city of Addison. Then read this: crime is a virus infecting the city of Addison. Same city, same crime numbers, one swapped word.

In a well-known 2011 study, that single word changed what people wanted to do. "Beast" pushed readers toward catching and caging: more police, tougher sentences. "Virus" pushed them toward treating the cause: find the conditions feeding it, fix what's spreading. Everyone saw identical statistics. The metaphor alone moved them about 18 points toward enforcement.

That's framing, and it steers your decisions more than you'd like to admit. "Tax relief" sounds like a gift. "Tax burden" sounds like a weight. Same policy, opposite gut reaction.

We wondered whether AI catches the same cold. Show a chatbot "crime is a beast," and does it lean toward the crackdown too?

We ran it on six models. They barely moved.

What we did

Plain version: we showed each model the crime report, swapped only the metaphor, and asked the same open question a human got. What should Addison do? Then we sorted each answer toward "enforce" or "address the causes," the same way the original study scored people.

We did this a lot. About 1,200 runs for the main test, and another 1,800 for the follow-up checks below.

Humans swung 18 points. The models swung somewhere between negative 9 and positive 9. That's noise. Wherever a person leaned hard, the AI sat still.

The model that called out the trick

Here's my favorite part. The strongest model didn't just resist the framing. It named it.

In some answers it wrote, in so many words, that the passage "uses persuasive techniques like the metaphor 'crime is a wild beast.'" It spotted the loaded word, flagged it as spin, and reasoned around it. The trick that works on us was, to it, a thing to point at.

The one "significant" result was a mirage

A quick honesty check, because it matters for how you read any AI study, including ours. Out of all those runs, a couple did cross into "statistically significant." Both came from the same model. When we read the actual answers behind the number, the two sides were nearly identical text. The score moved. The behavior didn't. It was a quirk in how the answers got tallied, not a real shift.

The lesson survives outside the lab. A number can cross a line while nothing real changed underneath. Read the outputs, not just the summary.

The reflex worth keeping Whenever an AI hands you a statistic, a confidence score, or a clean conclusion, read a few of the raw answers behind it before you act. A moving number is not the same as a moving result.

Did it just recognize the famous study?

Reasonable suspicion. These models have read most of the internet, and this experiment is on it. Maybe they recognized the 2011 passage and recited the "right" textbook answer.

We checked two ways. First, we asked them directly, and yes, the models often recognized the original. Then we rebuilt the whole test with brand-new metaphors they couldn't be parroting: crime as a wolf, crime as a cancer, set in a different city. Same flat result. The resistance held on material the models had never seen, so it isn't memorized politeness. It's a disposition.

Where the AI is weak, though

Resisting spin has a price, and you should know it. The models didn't just decline the bait. They mostly sat on the fence. They clustered on balanced "more policing and address the root causes" answers, and they landed well short of where humans went on actually picking a side.

Bias-resistance and wishy-washiness are the same move seen from two angles. The AI won't get yanked around by a loaded word. It also won't hand you a bold call unless you ask for one.

What this means for how you use AI

Here's the practical bit.

The big takeaway: an AI is often more useful as a check on your thinking than as a mirror of it. Our last piece showed it can absorb a bias you can't see. This one shows the other side. On loaded language, it's harder to push around than you are, and you can put that to work.

  1. Use it as a spin detector. Paste the slanted memo, the pitch, the headline, the heated Slack message, and ask: "What are the neutral facts here, and where is the wording steering me?" It's good at pulling those two apart.
  2. When you need a decision, force it. The same even-handedness that resists spin also produces fence-sitting. If you want a call, demand one: "Pick one side. Give me three concrete reasons. Tell me what you'd give up." Don't accept "it depends" when you asked for a verdict.
  3. Don't act on a summary number alone. Our one "significant" result was a scoring artifact. Spot-read the reasoning behind any stat or confidence score before you bet on it.
  4. Anchor it to your specifics. The model knows the famous frameworks, so on a well-known topic it may hand you the canonical answer. Push it onto your details: "Ignore the textbook version. Reason only from these facts: ..." Then it's working your problem, not a summary of someone else's.
Steal this prompt "Strip the framing out of this. List the neutral facts on one side, and on the other, point to the exact words doing the persuading. Then tell me what you'd conclude from the facts alone."

The bigger picture

Two experiments in, the pattern is already interesting. A worthless decoy option slipped a bias past the AI without it noticing. A loaded metaphor, the kind that reliably moves people, slid right off.

The difference is where the pressure came from. The decoy hid in the options. The metaphor sat in plain sight, in the words, and the AI is good at reading words with suspicion. It's worse at noticing the shape of the choices you lay in front of it.

A rough rule of thumb: trust an AI more to check your language, less to check your menu.
